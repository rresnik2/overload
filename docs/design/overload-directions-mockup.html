Four ways Overload could look

Each direction replaces the editorial paper-and-serif look and comes with a desktop Today screen and a mobile set logger. The phones are live. Scroll the rulers and wheels, turn the knobs, and flick the digit drums. On a laptop, drag with the mouse or click a control and use the arrow keys. Every direction uses the same data and keeps the existing rules: kg in storage, tabular figures and 44 px targets. A+B, the first section, is the mix you picked.

What the editorial style gets wrong in a gym

  • HighWeight entry is slowThe +/− stepper moves 2.5 kg per tap, so going from 82.5 to 100 kg takes seven taps mid-set.
  • HighIt reads like a magazineOn Today the italic word “Today” is the largest thing on screen, and the numbers you came for rank below it.
  • MediumThe figures are thinNewsreader digits look elegant on a desk. They are hard to read from a bench at arm’s length.
  • MediumEvery card looks the sameAll four Today cards share the same vermilion offset shadow, so no card stands out as the next thing to do.
  • LowThe look is commonCream paper, a serif display face and a red accent now appear on many products, so the app won’t look like its own.

A+B

Calibrated Dial

A’s rubber-floor palette and stamped type, with B’s rotary control rebuilt as a calibrated plate you turn. The picture above the dial follows the equipment: plates load onto a bar, dumbbells and kettlebells grow, and a machine stack lifts plate by plate.

Equipment visuals

  • Barbell: plates load per side as you turn, and the dial’s rim takes the colour of the heaviest plate on the bar.
  • Dumbbell: both heads grow with the weight per hand. The dial steps 2 kg, matching the app’s dumbbell default.
  • Kettlebell: the dial steps through real bell sizes (4 to 48 kg), and the bell grows and changes colour.
  • Machine: each click moves the pin down one plate, and the newly selected plates lift onto the carriage. The step comes from the machine’s pin step.

The dial

The dial is a calibrated plate seen face-on: a rubber rim with stamped ticks and a steel hub. The pointer stays still while the plate turns under it. One click is one step, every 12°.

Tap the big number to type a value, and it snaps to the nearest step for that equipment. Reps keep A’s number strip and RIR keeps five buttons.

Haptics

Intended feel: a light tick on every click of the dial, a tap tick on the RIR and equipment buttons, and a firmer double pulse on Log set.

Android would use the Vibration API and iOS the switch technique from ios-haptics. Neither has been tested on a device yet, so the mock may feel silent.

A

Calibrated

Based on competition plates. The background is dark like a rubber floor, the plate colours are the whole palette, and the condensed italic type looks like the stamping on a plate face. The weight control draws the bar you are about to load.

Palette and type

25 kg 20 kg 15 kg 10 kg 5 kg Floor

Archivo at 62% width, 850 italic, for displayArchivo at normal width for text. One family, two widths.

Entering numbers

Weight is a horizontal ruler that snaps every 2.5 kg. As it moves, the bar above reloads plates per side, so the plate math is done for you. Tap the big number to type a jump.

Reps use a number strip. RIR stays as five buttons, because five choices are quicker to tap than to scroll.

Trade-offs

  • Dark by default, which matches the gym default you already planned.
  • Lifters already know what the plate colours mean, so the palette carries information.
  • Five saturated colours need strict rules: use them for lifts and plates only, never for decoration.
  • Machines and cables have no plates. Their visual would be a weight-stack pin.
B

Bench Instrument

Styled as a piece of training hardware: a brushed aluminium panel, an OLED readout and colour-coded rotary encoders. Every number is set with a knob that clicks from step to step.

Palette and type

Panel OLED Weight Reps RIR Rest

Schibsted Grotesk for names and headingsMartian Mono for every label and number

Entering numbers

Each knob is an endless encoder: one click is one step. Weight moves 2.5 kg per click, reps 1, RIR 1. The colour of a knob matches the colour of its value on the screen.

The OLED shows the value at full size because your thumb covers the knob while you turn it.

Trade-offs

  • The most distinctive option, and it looks tactile even in screenshots.
  • Quick for small changes from last session’s numbers.
  • Circular dragging on glass is unfamiliar and imprecise. Knobs need to be large, and iOS Safari can’t vibrate, so each click needs clear visual feedback.
  • Designed light-first. A dark version needs its own panel and knob work.
C

Gym Clock

Based on the LED interval timer on the gym wall. Every number uses seven-segment digits, red for work and green for rest, readable from across the rack.

Palette and type

Board Work Rest Notice Text

Oxanium for labels and proseSeven-segment numbers are drawn shapes, not a font

Entering numbers

Each digit is its own drum, like a combination lock. Flick the tens drum to jump 10 kg, flick the ones drum for small changes, and use the last drum to switch between .0 and .5.

There is no step size to set up, and any weight is at most four flicks away.

Trade-offs

  • The easiest to read from a distance and in bright gyms. The rest timer becomes the main element.
  • A strong look that is clearly Overload’s own.
  • Dark only. Seven-segment digits can’t show prose, so settings and insights fall back to Oxanium and can look like a costume.
  • Screen readers should hear one weight value, not four separate digits.
D

Chalk

Soft, bright and close to a native app: a chalk-white background, rounded cards, a cobalt accent, and the iOS picker wheel people already know.

Palette and type

Chalk Ink Cobalt Chest Quads Shoulders

Bricolage Grotesque at display size for headingsBricolage Grotesque at text size for everything else

Entering numbers

Tap a set and a sheet slides up with three wheels: weight, reps and RIR. The wheels start at last session’s numbers, so repeating a set takes one tap on Log set.

This option takes the least learning of the four.

Trade-offs

  • The friendliest option for new lifters, with familiar gestures.
  • Easy to do well in both light and dark themes.
  • The least distinctive. It looks like many other fitness apps.
  • The sheet covers part of the set table, so it must close when you tap Log set.
